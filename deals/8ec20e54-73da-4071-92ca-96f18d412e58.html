The Five Kitchens — Why This Is the Headline

Most small motels at this price have zero cooking infrastructure. Adding it is the single most expensive line item in converting lodging to anything else, because you are running supply, waste, venting, and electrical to points in a building never designed to receive them. This property already has five.

For an operator, that unlocks the extended-stay market. Nightly motel rates in a small Oregon town are a grind. Weekly and monthly stays to hospital travelers and university visitors are not, and cooking facilities are what makes those bookings happen. Grande Ronde Hospital staffs traveling clinicians, and those contracts run thirteen weeks at a time.

For a converter, it means five locations in this building already have the wet work done.

Now the honest flip side, which is the same fact seen from the other end. The seller's explanation for why the kitchens are not in the rooms is that city code does not permit it. That rule is common, and the reason behind it is that a unit with cooking facilities generally stops being a lodging room and becomes a dwelling unit, which triggers a different occupancy classification, different building code requirements, and sometimes different zoning and parking standards. So the same rule that put those kitchens where they are is the rule that governs your conversion. Get the specific La Grande code section in writing before you plan around it. Ask the building department directly what it would take to add cooking facilities to a guest room, and what classification the building lands in afterward. That one conversation decides whether the conversion exit is cheap or expensive.

How to Read the $11,000 a Month

Eleven thousand dollars a month is gross revenue, reported by the seller from a prior operating period. It is not net operating income.

A small motel carries real expenses against that line: utilities across nine units, housekeeping, linens and supplies, insurance, property taxes, maintenance, labor, and online travel agency commissions that commonly run fifteen percent of bookings or more. Operating expenses on a property like this frequently absorb a substantial share of gross. Do not divide $132,000 by $375,000 and call it a return.

The property is also closed, so there is no current trailing twelve, no occupancy report, and no recent deposits to verify against. You are underwriting a restart.

Ask for what does exist before you contract: prior-year tax returns or Schedule E, transient lodging tax filings, any bookkeeping or property management system records, utility history, and the date the property actually stopped operating. The gap between that date and today drives how much work the restart is.

Things to Verify Before You Write an Offer

  • The exact La Grande code provision governing cooking facilities in lodging units, and what a change of use would require.
  • The configuration of the five kitchens: which are in shared or common areas, which serve specific units, and whether one of them is the owner's house.
  • How long the property has been closed, and whether lodging licenses, business registration, fire and health inspections, or Oregon transient lodging tax registration have lapsed.
  • Whether reopening triggers re-inspection or code upgrades with the City of La Grande or Union County.
  • The square footage split between the nine units, the owner's house, and the garage. At roughly 3,144 sq ft total, the individual units are small, which is normal for 1950 motel construction but matters to what you can do with them.
  • Current property tax assessment and insurance quotes, since insuring a closed lodging property differs from insuring an operating one.
  • Title, liens, and any existing franchise, management, or booking agreements.

Zoning and Conversion

The parcel is zoned LG-GC, General Commercial — the flexible end of the zoning map, supporting continued lodging use along with a range of commercial uses. Confirm the current permitted use list with the City of La Grande.

Separately, Oregon has moved aggressively on motel conversion. Under ORS 197.748, from 2021 legislation, local governments are required to allow conversion of a lawful hotel or motel use to an emergency shelter, and from there to affordable housing, notwithstanding ordinary land use regulations, where the property sits inside an urban growth boundary and is not on heavy industrial land. A conversion under that statute is not treated as a land use decision. Local governments may still apply building code, occupancy, siting, and design standards. Oregon has separately moved to ease conversion of commercially zoned buildings to housing more broadly, without a zone change.

None of that is a promise about this parcel, and the affordability conditions attached to the shelter and affordable housing pathway are real. Whether any of it applies here is a question for the City of La Grande and your own land use counsel. But if your model is conversion rather than lodging operation, Oregon is an unusually favorable state to be doing it in, this is an unusually cheap building to be doing it with, and five existing kitchens is an unusually good head start.
